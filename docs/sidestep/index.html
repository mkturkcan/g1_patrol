Sidestep: A Closed-Loop Benchmark of Foundation Models for Humanoid Navigation Among Pedestrians

Mehmet Kerem Turkcan

Paper (PDF) Supplement Repository G1 Patrol

Abstract

For robots and people to share everyday spaces, safe navigation through crowded, unstructured human environments is a prerequisite and the starting point for any form of human-robot coordination in the real world. Foundation models are increasingly proposed as general-purpose robot controllers, but whether they meet this requirement has not been established. As a first step, we introduce Sidestep, a closed-loop benchmark in which foundation models steer a simulated humanoid through New York City streets from an egocentric camera. We evaluate navigation, vision-language-action and general vision-language models against classical planners and reinforcement-learning policies. Specialized navigation models often fall below a blind goal-seeking baseline, and general vision-language models fail predominantly by colliding with pedestrians. Surprisingly, the same models localize pedestrians and recognize their walking direction, which indicates that they perceive pedestrians but do not anticipate their motion. We therefore propose a predictive affordance interface (PAI) that restricts a model's choices to motions forecast to remain free of collisions. The PAI substantially improves the success of most models, yet a gap to classical planners and to policies trained for the robot's sensors remains. We further study whether these findings hold when rendered views are replaced with realistic video generated in the control loop. Our results identify anticipation of human motion as a central open problem for robots that share space with people.

Main results

  • Navigation models. The five navigation-model configurations succeed in 5–14% of the goal-track test episodes. A goal seeker without perception succeeds in 19%.
  • General vision-language models. Given arrows for directions that are currently clear, the six models succeed in 24–42% of the episodes, and 29–38% of their episodes end in contact with a pedestrian.
  • Predictive affordance interface. The PAI raises the success of nine of ten models. The best model, Gemma 4 31B, rises from 34% to 62%, and its pedestrian contacts fall from 35% to 15%.
  • References. With the same inputs, a dynamic-window planner succeeds in 64% and ORCA in 67% of the episodes. Reinforcement-learning policies reach 66% with lidar, 68% with a depth camera and 71% with privileged state.
  • Realistic video in the loop. With the PAI, success on realistic video is 58.3% against 61.5% on renders for Qwen3.5-27B and 58.9% against 61.5% for Gemma 4 31B; neither difference is significant. EgoActor-8B, which acts on raw images, falls from 15.6% to 8.3%.

Benchmark

A simulated Unitree G1 humanoid walks with a learned locomotion policy that tracks velocity commands. Every controller sets these commands and none controls joints. The model under test receives images from the head camera and, on the goal track, the goal's position relative to the robot. An episode succeeds when the robot crosses the goal line. It fails on contact with a pedestrian or a structure, on entering the roadway outside a crosswalk, on a fall, or after 40 s. All controllers play the same seeded episodes.

Analysis

Effect of the Interface

The same model is evaluated through three interfaces. Arrows for currently clear directions help most models reach the goal but leave pedestrian contacts high. The PAI, which offers only motions forecast to stay clear, raises the success of nine of the ten models and lowers the pedestrian contacts of most. The exception is Hy-Embodied-VLM-1.0, which mostly selects the interface's single-step options.

Success and Pedestrian Contact

Controllers with the PAI and the references occupy the region of high success and low pedestrian contact. Navigation models have low rates of both, because most of their episodes end in structure contacts, roadway entries or time-outs.

Ablation of the Interface Components

A fractional factorial design switches the PAI's components on and off. Anticipation, the forecast of pedestrian motion, has the largest estimated effect on success (+8.6 points) and accounts for the reduction in pedestrian contacts (−16.1 points). Clearance from structures and the roadway map reduce structure contacts and roadway entries, respectively.

Closed-Loop Realism

Rendered images may disadvantage models trained on real imagery. The realism track therefore replaces each rendered view with realistic video: a causal video model transfers the robot's view as it is rendered, and the model under test acts on the transfer while the simulator keeps the state and scores the episode.

Relation to Sidewalk Navigation Benchmarks

Two recent works study the same setting. SidewalkBench (Liu et al., CoRL 2026) evaluates nine visual navigation models on procedurally generated and scanned sidewalk scenes and reports that pedestrian interaction remains a bottleneck. FlowPilot (He et al., 2026) is a monocular navigation policy pretrained on delivery-robot fleet data and tuned with human interventions. Both are stronger than Sidestep in scene diversity, route length, scanned real scenes and real-robot tests. Sidestep addresses different questions:

  • Which model families are tested. Sidestep evaluates general vision-language models, embodied reasoning models and vision-language-action models in addition to navigation models. CityWalker and InternVLA-N1 appear in both benchmarks, and the low success of navigation models here is consistent with SidewalkBench.
  • What the task permits. Planners and reinforcement-learning policies run on the same episodes, with and without privileged state. A model's score is therefore read against what a non-learned planner or a sensor-trained policy achieves, which neither prior work reports.
  • Why the models fail. SidewalkBench quantifies failures and states that it does not analyze their causes. Sidestep probes perception directly and uses a factorial intervention, which attributes the dominant failure to missing anticipation of pedestrian motion.
  • A remedy without training. SidewalkBench and FlowPilot improve models by fine-tuning on synthetic or intervention data. The PAI changes only the interface and applies to any decision maker.
  • Embodiment and pedestrians. The robot is a humanoid with a learned walking policy. Pedestrians react to the robot through a social-force model and a fraction of them are inattentive; SidewalkBench scripts eight behaviors triggered by the robot's position.
  • Realism. Sidestep streams generative video into the control loop on the same episodes, which is complementary to SidewalkBench's Gaussian-splatting scans.
  • Statistics. Every configuration is reported with a confidence interval, and comparisons use paired tests on identical episodes.

Limitations

All results are in simulation. The PAI's inputs other than the camera tracker are ideal (the simulator's tracks and obstacles, exact depth and localization), and the simulation pauses while a model decides. Pedestrians follow a social-force model with scripted routes. The realism track uses one video model. Routes are short, and the benchmark covers one humanoid, one city and open models only.
