G1 Patrol: Humanoid Navigation in Dense Urban Pedestrian Environments

Mehmet Kerem Turkcan

Code Video BibTeX

Abstract

We study goal-directed navigation of a Unitree G1 humanoid in dense urban pedestrian environments. We construct a procedurally generated suite of New York City street scenarios (sidewalks, sidewalk sheds, crosswalks, construction zones, doorways and pedestrian plazas) at ten difficulty levels, populated by pedestrians that follow a social-force model in which some pedestrians react to the robot only at close range. Control is hierarchical: a locomotion policy trained with the actuator limits of the physical robot tracks velocity commands at 50 Hz, and a navigation policy issues these commands at 10 Hz through a tanh mapping and a low-pass filter. A navigation teacher with privileged state is trained with PPO under a success-based curriculum and distilled into student policies that observe only the robot's onboard sensors (Livox MID-360 lidar or Intel RealSense D435i depth camera) through height maps accumulated over 3 s. Over all 120 scenario tiles, the teacher reaches the goal in 74.9% of episodes, the lidar student in 69.3% and the camera student in 69.1%, with falls in at most 0.2% of episodes. Compared with an earlier teacher that outputs clamped commands (74.0% success), the share of time with lateral commands above 0.25 m/s decreases from 97% to 19% and the fall rate from 1.1% to 0.2%. Success is lowest on the narrowest sidewalks (18–23% on hard tiles). All experiments are conducted in simulation (Isaac Lab 3.0, Isaac Sim 6.1).

Method

The navigation policy outputs a velocity command (forward velocity, lateral velocity and yaw rate) at 10 Hz. The output is mapped into the command ranges by a tanh (forward −0.4 to 1.0 m/s, lateral ±0.4 m/s, yaw rate ±1.0 rad/s) and low-pass filtered: at each step the command moves halfway to the requested value. A frozen locomotion policy converts the command into joint position targets for the 37 joints of the G1 at 50 Hz, which are tracked by PD controllers; the simulator runs at 200 Hz. The teacher observes privileged simulator state. Student policies observe proprioception, the goal position and one onboard sensor, and are trained to reproduce the teacher's commands.

Training proceeds in three stages; each stage is frozen before the next is trained (Figure 4, Table 1). The locomotion policy is trained on uneven city terrain with the torque and velocity limits of the G1 actuators, gait-phase and posture rewards, and random pushes. The teacher is trained with PPO on the scenario suite under an adaptive curriculum, initialized from an earlier teacher whose output layer is scaled by 0.3; the collision-risk reward (Table 2) is added after 2,050 iterations and training continues for 1,200 iterations, and the last 1,000 iterations use the path-based pedestrian model (earlier stages used straight lanes inside the tile). The students are trained by distillation from the teacher.

The curriculum keeps an exponential moving average of the success rate on each tile. A level is unlocked once the next-easier level of the same tile column reaches 50% success. When an episode ends, the robot's next tile is drawn with a uniformly random scenario type and a level drawn from the unlocked levels with probability proportional to s(1 − s) + 0.02, where s is the tile's success rate; this concentrates training on levels the policy solves about half of the time. A fifth of the draws are uniform over the unlocked levels and 5% uniform over all levels. During distillation the curriculum follows the student's success rate.

Environment

The terrain is a grid of 16 m × 10 m tiles. Columns correspond to the six scenario types and rows to ten difficulty levels. In each episode the robot is placed at one end of a route on its tile and must reach the other end within 40 s.

Pedestrian model

  • Dynamics. Pedestrians are kinematic bodies that follow walking paths under a social-force model with repulsion from other pedestrians and from structures. Walking speeds are drawn from 0.7–1.3 m/s at level 1 and 0.8–1.6 m/s at level 10; 10% walk at 0.4–0.6 m/s.
  • Encounters. 65% of walking pedestrians are timed to reach the robot's route when the robot does (assuming 0.8 m/s), so that pedestrians cross and block the route. The number of pedestrians increases with difficulty, up to 20 on plaza tiles.
  • Awareness. A fraction of pedestrians (90% at level 1, 60% at level 10) perceive the robot: they keep a clearance of at least 0.1 m, slow down when the robot blocks their path and step around it. The resulting loss of pedestrian speed is penalized, which rewards yielding.
  • Distracted pedestrians. The remaining pedestrians do not slow down and react only within 1 m with a lateral swerve.
  • Entering and leaving. Each walking path leads from where people enter the scene, 45 m beyond the tile's ends, out of a shop door or up a subway stairwell, to 45 m beyond the tile's ends, so pedestrians do not appear or disappear in view. Walkers that have left re-enter on a new path. A walker is never pushed backwards along its path; it may slow down, stop or step sideways.
  • Other behaviors. Stationary pedestrians at waiting spots (curbs, doorways, work zones) start walking after 5–25 s. No pedestrian is spawned within 1.5 m of the robot.
  • Feasibility. A unit test verifies that every route admits a path of width 0.6 m on every tile type at difficulties 0, 0.5, 0.8 and 1.

Sensing

The student policies use only the sensors installed on the G1, at the poses given in the robot's URDF. The MID-360 is mounted inverted in the head, about 1.1 m above the ground, with a vertical field of view of −52° to +7°; it is simulated with 32 channels at 1° horizontal resolution and a 10 m range. The D435i is pitched 47.6° downward.

Each student receives a height map built from its sensor's returns (Figure 9). Returns are binned into a world-aligned grid that moves with the robot; the robot's motion between scans is taken from the simulator, in place of lidar-inertial or visual-inertial odometry. A cell holds the highest return of its latest observation and is cleared 3 s after it was last observed. The policy receives this accumulated map together with the current and the previous scan, resampled into the robot's heading frame; the camera student additionally receives the last three depth images.

Results

All navigation policies are evaluated with the curriculum disabled and robots distributed uniformly over all 120 tiles (2,400 robots, 60 s of simulated time, PhysX). Pedestrian motion and policy inputs are as in training; random pushes are disabled. The earlier teacher is evaluated on the same tiles with its own command interface: outputs clamped to forward −0.6 to 1.2 m/s, lateral ±0.6 m/s and yaw rate ±1.2 rad/s, without filtering.

The lidar and camera students reach 93% and 92% of the teacher's success rate. The largest differences are on construction-zone tiles of medium difficulty (75% for the teacher, 51% and 47% for the students), where the clear walkway is a chicane of fences and barriers (Figure 10). Two changes between the first and the final students, accumulating the height map over 3 s and computing the distillation loss on tanh-mapped actions, reduced obstacle contacts of the camera student from 24.4% to 12.3% and increased its success rate from 60.4% to 69.8%; the success rate of the lidar student increased from 70.0% to 72.1% (both measured with the earlier pedestrian model, before fine-tuning on the path-based one). Pedestrian contact is the most frequent failure for all policies. On hard sidewalk tiles, where the walkway admits one person at a time, success is 18–23%.

Limitations

  • All results are in simulation; the policies have not been deployed on hardware.
  • The students' height maps use the simulated robot motion in place of an odometry estimate; odometry drift is not modeled.
  • The locomotion policy observes a terrain height scan around the robot, which is not provided by the G1's sensors.
  • Pedestrians are kinematic and scripted; contact ends the episode, and physical interaction between robot and pedestrians is not modeled.
  • Success on the narrowest corridors remains low: 18–23% on hard sidewalk tiles and 47–56% on hard sidewalk-shed tiles.
  • With the default mounting, the D435i observes the ground only up to about 3.4 m ahead, which limits anticipation for a camera-only policy.

Context

This project is part of work toward a civic robot system for the applications of the Center for Smart Streetscapes (CS3), an NSF Engineering Research Center led by Columbia University. It follows earlier work on street-scene perception: SyntheticStreetScenes, a dataset of approximately 5,200 generated street-scene videos with prompts and attributes, and StreetPrompt, a street-camera video classifier with classes specified as text.
